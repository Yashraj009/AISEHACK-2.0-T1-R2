1. Dataset & Findings
The task is to estimate crop health and yield-to-date for every farm in Sokhda village, Vadodara, using Synthetic
Aperture Radar. SAR is the right sensor for this problem: the kharif season runs through the monsoon, when
cloud creates optical time series full of gaps, and SAR sees through cloud because it supplies its own illumination.
The data comprises of 4 Capella X-band Single Look Complex (SLC) acquisitions 6 June, 19 June, 14 August
and 13 October 2025 with a cadastral layer of 966 farm polygons covering 447.5 ha and the village boundary.
All four are single-polarisation (HH), with incidence angles from 28.7◦to 35.2◦; scene geometry, scale factor
and noise floor follow the product definitions in [1]. Sokhda is the same village analysed in Round 1 and carries
the same identifier in the shapefile, so the crop areas established there are carried forward as a quantitative
constraint on crop assignment. Round 1 delivered areas rather than plot counts, so the areas are treated as
measurements and the implied counts as an assumption. Sentinel-2 imagery [2] is used only as an independent
reference, never as an input to the submitted columns.
Figure 1 shows the farm geometry against the sensor resolution. This matters because X-band uses a wavelength
of roughly 3cm, which penetrates a canopy only slightly [3], so the return describes the top of the crop and the
soil roughness beneath it rather than total biomass. With a median plot of 0.27 ha, the first question is whether
a field contains enough independent radar cells to measure at all. As the right panel of Figure 1 shows, even
small plots span many cells at the 2.94 m processing grid.
Figure 1. Farm geometry and its relationship to sensor resolution. The village holds 966 digitised plots covering 38% of

Four properties of the imagery affect any measurement built on it, and each is handled explicitly. First, the
supplied 8-bit preview pushes 30-37% of farm pixels onto a single value, and the proportion differs between dates,
so all processing uses the raw SLC. Second, the village sits near the edge of the radar swath, and plots crossing
that edge return an elevated signal that varies between dates; 99-100% of the small-sample and temporal-jump
flags fall on these same plots, so one geometric cause explains three apparent problems. Third, mean backscatter
rises with plot area on all four dates (r up to +0.27), so every feature is corrected for size. Fourth, a single
bright object inside a boundary a metal roof or a vehicle shifts that field’s mean by as much as 19 dB.
2. Methodology
The pipeline consists of five stages: calibration and geocoding, speckle suppression, per-farm feature extraction,
crop assignment, and computation of the two output indices.

2.3 Crop assignment
Round 1 area totals are imposed as a hard constraint, and plots are ordered by November Sentinel-2 greenness
[2, 8] cotton is picked October to January, so it stays green when the other crops are bare. Clustering was
tested and rejected: silhouette falls from 0.525 at two groups to 0.154 at five, and clusters carry no labels
identifying which is cotton. Rice adds a difficulty no processing removes, since its X-band signature is the flooded
transplanting stage [9] and no acquisition falls in that window.
Matching area alone leaves plot count unconstrained, which produced one-hectare maize fields in a village whose
median plot is 0.27ha. The allocator therefore scores every plot-crop pair with two controls, a price pc driving
area and a size preference γc driving count:
si,c = Li,c + pc + γc z(ln Ai)
(4)
with Li,c the optical preference. Prices and preferences are updated until area error falls below 1% and count
error below 12%; every crop lands within 0.50ha of target while also matching its implied plot count.

No ground truth exists for these fields, so validity is established against Sentinel-2 [2], which enters neither
submitted column, and against nulls built to be difficult to beat. Backscatter agrees with October NDVI [8]
at ρ = +0.622 across 879 plots, rising to +0.742 on plots above 0.8ha, and a five-fold out-of-sample model
predicts greenness on unseen plots at R2 = 0.621. As Figure 3 shows, agreement improves with plot size, which
is what speckle theory predicts [3], because larger fields contain more independent looks. Removing the optical
features entirely and rebuilding the map from radar alone separates November greenness at H = 115 against a
constraint-only null of 12; the resulting classification is shown in Figure 4. The per-plot label confidence was
tested against that same radar-only map, and agreement rises across confidence quartiles from 30.6% to 61.2%.
The yield driver scores +0.540 against integrated NDVI, +0.219 better than the health index alone.

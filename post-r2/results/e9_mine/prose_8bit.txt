# Reading the X-band Canopy: Crop Health and Yield-to-Date for 966 Farms from Capella SLC

### ANRF AISEHack 2.0 — Round 2 | Team 8bit | Sokhda village, Vadodara

This notebook builds two farm-level indices for every one of the 966 digitised plots in
Sokhda village from four Capella X-band SLC acquisitions (6 Jun, 19 Jun, 14 Aug, 13 Oct 2025):

* **X-CHI** — an X-band Canopy Health Index, scored *within crop class*
* **RYPI** — Relative Yield Potential as observed through 13 October

Everything downstream of the raw SLC is built here from the Capella extended metadata and the
RPC model — calibration, datum resolution, geocoding, interferometric baselines, coherence and
zonal statistics. No SNAP, no ISCE, no `gdalwarp`. That is deliberate: every radiometric and
geometric step is visible and auditable rather than hidden inside a toolbox default.

### The three findings that shaped the method

**1. The four scenes were not flown at a common incidence angle** (35.24°, 28.77°, 28.69°,
31.53°). Backscatter depends steeply on incidence, and differently for soil than for canopy,
so no normalisation removes this exactly. Computed naively, "August minus June" canopy gain
comes out at **−0.23 dB** — peak-season fields appearing *darker* than bare pre-monsoon soil,
which is not credible. The geometry contamination is the same size as the crop signal.
The fix drives the whole design: **19 Jun and 14 Aug are 0.08° apart**, so their difference is
bias-free, and the index is anchored there.

**2. Interferometry is not available on this dataset, and we prove it rather than assume it.**
Perpendicular baselines computed from the precise state vectors show five of six pairs exceed
the critical baseline by 2–4×. The one valid pair (19 Jun / 14 Aug, B⊥ = 833 m vs 15,151 m
critical) is separated by 56 days of monsoon growth and decorrelates to a median of 0.02.

**3. Sub-look spectral coherence and GLCM texture were computed in full and then
down-weighted or dropped** — sub-look farm means span only 0.033–0.037, inside estimator
noise, and GLCM contrast/entropy/homogeneity track mean brightness at ρ ≈ ±0.55, i.e. they
are γ⁰ under another name. Both are reported here rather than quietly deleted.

## 1 · Core SAR library

Implemented from first principles against the Capella extended metadata:

* **Radiometric calibration** — `beta0 = (|DN| * scale_factor)^2`, with the thermal noise power
  (NESZ, −26 to −28 dB depending on scene) subtracted so that dark pre-monsoon fields are not
  credited with backscatter that is really instrument noise. The sign of the exponent is
  *verified*, not assumed: the multiplicative form puts the scene median at −18 dB with the 5th
  percentile landing exactly on the stated NESZ floor, whereas the divisive form gives +87 dB.
* **Exact per-pixel incidence angle** from the 108 precise state vectors, rather than the
  scene-constant metadata value — the AOI spans ~0.6° of incidence.
* **RPC00B ground-to-image projection**, written out explicitly.
* **Datum resolution** — Capella RPCs expect ellipsoidal heights, Copernicus DEM stores
  orthometric. The offset is *solved* from the 225 GCPs embedded in each SLC instead of guessed.
* **Interferometric baselines**, orbit-driven coregistration, complex resampling, coherence.
* **Speckle-corrected CV** — `CV_true² = CV_obs² − 1/ENL`, using an *empirical* ENL, because
  geocoding correlates neighbouring pixels and the nominal look count is not the effective one.

## 2 · Preprocessing: calibration, datum, geocoding

The AOI is only 5.5 × 4.4 km, so rather than geocoding 2.25 GB of full scenes we invert the
RPC model to find the exact SLC subset that projects onto the target grid and read only that.
Output is terrain-referenced γ⁰ on a common 1.5 m UTM 43N grid, plus the complex slant-range
chip retained for the interferometric stages (coherence must be estimated *before* any
resampling, or interpolation destroys the phase).

## 3 · Is interferometry even possible here? (a rigorous negative result)

Rather than assuming repeat-pass coherence will work, we compute the perpendicular baseline for
all six date pairs from the precise state vectors and compare against the critical baseline.
<details>
<summary><b>Design notes — <code>02_coherence.py</code></b> (click to expand)</summary>

```
Stage 2 — Repeat-pass interferometric coherence in slant geometry.

Why this stage exists
---------------------
Backscatter amplitude alone cannot separate "bright because the canopy is dense"
from "bright because the soil is wet". Interferometric coherence is a completely
independent observable: it measures whether the *arrangement of scatterers*
inside a resolution cell survived between two passes. A crop canopy that grows
between two dates randomises those scatterers and drives coherence to the noise
floor; bare soil, fallow, roads and buildings stay coherent.

So coherence *loss* is an amplitude-independent detector of canopy development,
and residual coherence flags plots that never developed a canopy at all.

Only pairs that are geometrically capable of interfering are used. The
perpendicular baseline is computed from the precise state vectors and compared
against the critical baseline; pairs that fail are reported and discarded rather
than silently producing noise.

Outputs (work/):
    coh_<pairname>.tif       coherence, geocoded to the common AOI grid
    coherence_report.json    baselines, coregistration residuals, usability
```

</details>
## 4 · Sub-look spectral coherence

With repeat-pass interferometry ruled out, the remaining way to use the SLC *phase* is within a
single acquisition: split the processed range bandwidth into two disjoint sub-bands and measure
their complex correlation. Deterministic scatterers respond identically at both frequencies;
a random canopy volume does not.

**Outcome, reported honestly:** the farm-level means span only 0.033–0.037 across all 966 plots
— inside the estimator's own noise. The physics is unsurprising (two disjoint sub-bands over
fully developed speckle are independent by construction), and the feature is therefore excluded
from the final index rather than dressed up as signal.
<details>
<summary><b>Design notes — <code>02b_sublook.py</code></b> (click to expand)</summary>

```
Stage 2b — Sub-look (spectral) coherence: a per-date measure of how much of the
backscatter comes from *coherent* scatterers versus a *distributed volume*.

Motivation
----------
Stage 2 showed that repeat-pass interferometry is a dead end for this dataset:
five of the six date pairs exceed the critical baseline by 2-4x, and the one
geometrically valid pair (19 Jun / 14 Aug, Bperp = 833 m) is separated by 56
days of monsoon crop growth, which decorrelates X-band completely. Median
debiased coherence came out at 0.02.

But the SLC phase is still usable -- just *within* a single acquisition.
Splitting the processed range bandwidth into two sub-bands produces two looks of
the same scene at different frequencies. Their complex correlation behaves very
differently for the two scattering regimes we care about:

  * a deterministic scatterer (bund, building, rough dry clod, stubble row)
    responds identically at both frequencies  -> sub-look coherence stays HIGH
  * a random volume of canopy elements produces frequency-dependent constructive
    and destructive interference                -> sub-look coherence goes LOW

So low sub-look coherence is a direct, amplitude-independent indicator that a
resolution cell is filled with a distributed vegetation volume rather than a
bare or structurally simple surface. Unlike repeat-pass coherence it needs no
coregistration, no baseline, and it is available on all four dates.

This is the standard "coherent vs incoherent scattering" decomposition; here it
does the job that a cross-polarised channel would do if Capella had flown one.

Outputs (work/):
    sublook_coh_<date>.tif     geocoded sub-look coherence per date
    sublook_report.json
```

</details>
## 5 · Farm-level feature extraction

Two details that matter more than they look:

* **Polygons are eroded by 2.5 m.** With a median plot of 0.27 ha at 1.5 m sampling, field
  bunds and access tracks would otherwise contaminate a large fraction of every plot.
* **Statistics are taken on linear γ⁰ and converted to dB afterwards.** Averaging dB values
  averages logarithms of power, which is biased.

<details>
<summary><b>Design notes — <code>04_indices.py</code></b> (click to expand)</summary>

```
Stage 4 — Round 1 carry-forward, crop assignment, health index, yield index.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
The constraint that shaped this entire stage: acquisition geometry
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
The four scenes were NOT flown at a common incidence angle:

    6 Jun  35.24 deg     19 Jun  28.77 deg
   14 Aug  28.69 deg     13 Oct  31.53 deg

Backscatter depends steeply on incidence angle, and the dependence is different
for bare soil than for a canopy, so no single normalisation (sigma0, gamma0 or a
cosine power law) can remove it exactly for a mixed scene. A naive
"August minus June" canopy metric is therefore contaminated by a 6.5 deg
geometry change, and in this dataset that contamination is the same size as the
crop signal itself: computed that way, the mean canopy gain at peak vegetative
comes out at -0.23 dB, i.e. peak-season fields appear *darker* than bare
pre-monsoon soil, which is not physically credible.

The fix is to let the geometry dictate the analysis rather than fight it:

  * 19 Jun and 14 Aug are 0.08 deg apart -- effectively the same geometry.
    Their difference is a clean, bias-free measure of canopy establishment
    across the main vegetative period. (This is also the only pair that passed
    the interferometric baseline test in Stage 2 -- the same orbital repeat is
    what makes it both radiometrically and geometrically comparable.)
  * 13 Oct is 2.84 deg from 14 Aug -- a small, second-order correction.
  * 6 Jun sits 6.5 deg away and is used only as a qualitative bare-soil
    descriptor, never inside a cross-date difference that feeds the index.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
What was tested and dropped, and why
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Two complex-domain products were computed in full and then excluded from the
index because they carry no dynamic range at farm level -- reported here rather
than quietly deleted:

  * repeat-pass coherence (19 Jun / 14 Aug): farm means span 0.032 to 0.052
  * sub-look spectral coherence:             farm means span 0.033 to 0.037

Both spreads sit inside the estimator's own noise, so neither can rank fields.
The physics is unsurprising: 56 days of monsoon canopy growth destroys X-band
repeat-pass correlation, and two disjoint sub-bands over fully developed speckle
are independent by construction.

GLCM texture was also computed and then down-weighted: contrast, entropy and
homogeneity all track the field mean brightness almost exactly, so including
them at full weight would have been double-counting gamma0 under another name.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Part A: carrying Round 1 forward
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Round 1 produced *village-level crop acreage* for 29 villages, not per-plot
labels, and the Round 2 farm polygons are a fresh digitisation with their own
FIDs. There is no join key, so a merge on FID would be meaningless.

What Round 1 does provide is the crop area composition of Sokhda (village 22),
and that is a genuine constraint: whatever per-plot labelling we produce, the
areas have to add up. So the carry-forward is an area-constrained assignment --
score every plot against a phenological template, then solve for the assignment
minimising total mismatch subject to the Round 1 areas. That uses Round 1
exactly as far as it is trustworthy and lets the Round 2 SAR pick which plot
gets which label.
```

## 7 · Auxiliary data — rainfall context and an independent optical check

Both free and equally available to every participant; both *support* the SAR rather than
replace it.

The rainfall result matters: **14 Aug (3.8 mm in the prior 3 days) and 13 Oct (0.0 mm) are both

**The crop-level physics came out right without being fitted to anything.** The template
matching was given phenological priors, but the measured backscatter behaviour it produced is
independently agronomically correct for a mid-October observation in central Gujarat:

* **Cotton is the only crop still rising in October (+1.37 dB).** Correct — it is the one
  long-duration crop here, with bolls still developing and picking not starting until November.
* **Bajra is lowest in October and falling fastest (−1.91 dB, October level −20.78 dB).**
  Correct — a 75–85 day crop sown in late June is harvested by late September, so by 13 October
  those plots are effectively back to bare soil.
* **Groundnut shows the weakest canopy establishment of all (−4.40 dB).** Correct — a prostrate
  low-biomass legume never builds a canopy that dominates an X-band return; the signal stays
  soil-driven all season.
* **Maize sits between**, peaking in August and senescing by October (−1.06 dB).

**X-CHI is crop-relative by construction, RYPI is village-relative.** Mean X-CHI is ≈50 for
every crop *on purpose*: the question "is this field doing well?" only means something against
what that crop should be doing. A groundnut plot cannot be penalised for not scattering like
rice. RYPI is scaled across the whole village instead, so genuine productivity differences
survive — and they do: rice (70.6) and maize (69.6) rank well above groundnut (33.7).

**Where the index is weakest, stated plainly.** Agreement with Sentinel-2 is strong for cotton
(ρ = +0.62) and groundnut (+0.44) but near zero for bajra (+0.01). That is expected rather than
alarming: bajra was harvested weeks before 13 October, so *both* sensors are looking at bare
soil and there is no canopy condition left for either to measure. It is a limitation of
observing on 13 October, not a failure of the index — and it is exactly why the deliverable is
framed as yield **to date** rather than a harvest forecast.

**Coverage.** All 966 plots carry a village_id, crop_type, health_index and
yield_estimate_to_date. 883 are flagged `ok`; 70 lack usable four-date SAR statistics (they fall
in the AOI corner outside the SLC swath), 9 have partial coverage and 4 are too small to erode.
Those flags ship in the CSV rather than being hidden.

Templates use five geometry-safe features: γ⁰ level on 19 Jun
(monsoon-onset response; puddled rice is specular and dark
while dry-sown fields stay bright), 14 Aug − 19 Jun
(establishment, identical geometry), 13 Oct − 14 Aug (retention vs

DO THE CROP LABELS HOLD UP?
This is the weakest link — everything downstream depends
on it and there is no ground truth. But the labels never see
optical data, so they can be tested against Sentinel-2: arbitrary
labels cannot separate an independent variable. They separate
it decisively — Kruskal–Wallis H = 243, p = 2×10⁻⁵¹ — in
exactly the Kharif order: Rice 0.362 > Cotton 0.352 > Maize
0.287 > Groundnut 0.268 > Bajra 0.202. Standing crops high,
harvested crops low, bajra lowest by a clear margin.

THE CORE ARGUMENT
In SAR terms “brighter” is not “healthier” — it depends on
where the crop should be in its cycle on 13 October. For cotton
a rising October signal is healthy; for bajra the identical
behaviour means the field was not harvested on time or is
weed-dominated. Expected Oct−Aug retention is set per crop
(Cotton +1.4, Rice +0.3, Groundnut −0.3, Maize −0.6, Bajra −1.0
dB) and scored as a distance, not a magnitude.
Caveat, stated plainly. The establishment term contains both
canopy growth (+) and soil-moisture decline (−): 109 mm fell in
the three days before the 19 Jun pass. That storm was village-
wide, so the moisture term is largely common-mode and
differences between fields still reflect differential canopy
development.
8 RYPI — yield to date
Yield is accumulated biomass that reached grain or boll fill. Three
multiplicative terms, because they compensate rather than add
— a plot that built no canopy is not rescued by a tidy senescence
curve, and one that collapsed early never converted what it built.
RYPI ∝ ACC0.55 × CON0.30 × EVN0.15
ACC is the trapezoidal integral of canopy gain relative to the 19
Jun sowing-state across the two geometry-comparable later dates
— the SAR analogue of integrated NDVI, the standard biomass-
accumulation proxy. CON is phenological conformity. EVN is
evenness: yield is an area integral, and patchy fields lose the
patches. RYPI is scaled across the whole village, not within crop,
so real productivity differences survive. Health asks “is this field
doing well for its crop?”; yield asks “how much comes off this
field relative to the village?”
9 External datasets
All free and openly accessible; all complement the SAR rather
than replace it. Copernicus DEM GLO-30 for geocoding and the
datum solution. NASA POWER daily precipitation for moisture
context — critically, 14 Aug (3.8 mm prior 3 days) and 13 Oct (0.0
mm) are both dry, so the two dates the index leans on are canopy-
dominated rather than moisture-inflated; only 19 Jun is wet, and
it is used for classification and as the sowing reference, not as a
vigour measure. Sentinel-2 L2A (Microsoft Planetary Computer)
for validation only — it never enters the index.

The crop-level physics came out right without being fitted to
anything. The templates supplied phenological priors, but the
measured behaviour they produced is independently correct for a
mid-October observation in central Gujarat:
Cotton is the only crop still rising in October (+1.37 dB) — the
one long-duration crop, bolls still developing, picking from
November.
Bajra is lowest and falling fastest (−1.91 dB; Oct level −20.78
dB, closest to bare soil) — a 75–85 day crop sown late June is
harvested by late September.
Groundnut shows the weakest establishment (−4.40 dB) — a
prostrate low-biomass legume never builds a canopy that
dominates an X-band return.
Maize sits between, peaking in August and senescing by
October.
Village aggregation is area-weighted, not a plain mean over plots
— plot sizes span three orders of magnitude here. Sokhda: area-
weighted X-CHI 50.6, RYPI 48.2. By area, 276 ha (62%) falls in the
moderate-to-good bands, 47 ha in the severe band and 67 ha in
the vigorous band.
Spatial pattern. The health map shows coherent clustering rather
than plot-level noise — a contiguous belt of high-scoring plots
along the south-east and a lower-scoring block through the
village centre — which is what genuine agronomic variation
looks like and what a speckle-driven artefact would not produce.
WHERE THE INDEX IS WEAKEST
Agreement with Sentinel-2 is strong for cotton and groundnut
but near zero for bajra (ρ = +0.01). Expected rather than
alarming: bajra was harvested weeks before 13 October, so
both sensors see bare soil and no canopy condition remains for
either to measure. A limitation of observing on 13 October —
and precisely why the deliverable is framed as yield to date
rather than a harvest forecast.
Coverage. All 966 plots carry village_id, farm_id, crop_type,
health_index and yield_estimate_to_date. 883 are flagged ok ; 70
lack usable four-date statistics (they fall in the AOI corner outside
the SLC swath), 9 have partial coverage, 4 are too small to erode.
Flags ship in the results workbook rather than being hidden.
12 Reproducibility
The public notebook runs the full chain from raw SLC to CSV
from a fresh restart and ends with an assertion cell that verifies
the regenerated file matches the required five-column format
exactly. Path resolution searches for the shapefiles rather than
hard-coding a layout, so it runs unchanged on Kaggle and locally.
All external data is fetched programmatically from free, no-
authentication endpoints.
13 Next step
One more acquisition at 28.7° would make the whole four-date
series radiometrically comparable end to end, roughly doubling
the usable temporal signal. Failing that, an angular normalisation
fitted across a wider AOI — spanning several degrees of
incidence rather than 0.6° — would let the 6 June bare-soil
reference back into the index, which is the largest single piece of
information the geometry currently forces us to leave on the table.
